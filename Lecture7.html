Concepts in Probability

What is probability?

Probability measures the likelihood of an outcome. It ranges from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).

\[ 0 \leq P(A) \leq 1\\ \]

  • Experiment: a process with uncertain outcomes (e.g. rolling a die)

  • Sample space (S): the collection of all possible outcomes of a random experiment

  • Event: any subset of basic outcomes from the sample space

  • Basic outcome: a possible outcome of a random experiment

# Example using R
S <- 1:6  # sample space for rolling a die
A <- c(2, 4, 6)  # event: rolling an even number
B <- c(4, 5, 6)  # event: rolling an even number

Event relationships

As discussed on lecture 1, if A and B are two events in a sample space S:

Union (A ∪ B)

If A and B are two events in a sample space S, then union, A U B, is the set of all outcomes in S that belong to either A or B

Intersection (A ∩ B)

If A and B are two events in a sample space S, then A ∩ B is the set of all outcomes in S that belong to both A and B

Event relationships

Mutually exclusive

A and B cannot both occur - there is no outcome in common, i.e A ∩ B is empty

Complement (\(\bar{A}\) or A’)

The Complement of an event A is the set of all basic outcomes in the sample space that do not belong to A.So, it is the set of all outcomes in S not in A.

Classical Probability

Definitions of probability. Assumes all outcomes in the sample space are equally likely to occur.

Classical

Based on logical reasoning

\[ P(A)= \frac{N_A}{N}=\frac{number \ of \ outcomes \ that \ satisfy \ the \ event \ A}{total \ number \ of \ outcomes \ in \ the \ sample \ space} \\ \]

Relative frequency

Based on observed data. The limit of the proportion of times that an event A occurs in a large number of trial, \(n\)

\[ P(A)= \frac{N_A}{N}=\frac{number \ of \ events \ in \ the \ population \ that \ satisfy \ A}{total \ number \ of \ events \ in \ the \ population } \\ \]

Subjective

Based on personal judgment. It expresses an individual’s opinion or belief about the probability of occurence.

Number of combinations

Formula to compute the number of combinations of \(n\) items taken \(x\) at a time: \[ C_{x}^n =\frac{n!}{x!(n - x)!} \]

Factorial (!): the total number of possible ways of arranging \(x\) objects in order.

The factorial of a positive integer \(x\), denoted \(x!\), is defined as:

\[ x! = x (x-1) (x-2) \cdots (2) (1) \]

By convention: \(0! = 1\)

Classical Probability - Permutations and combinations

Permutations

The number of possible arrangements when \(x\) objects are to be selected from a total of \(n\) objects (i.e. \(P_x^n\)) and arranged in order (with \(n-x\)) objects left over

order matters

this means that \(n(n-1)(n-2)...(n-x+1)\) so that \((n-x)\) objects are left over,

multiplying and dividing the right hand side by \((n-x)(n-x-1)...(2)(1)=(n-x)!\) give us

\[ P_x^n =\frac{n(n-1)(n-2)...(n-x+1)(n-x)(n-x-1)...(2)(1)}{(n-x)(n-x-1)...(2)(1)} \\ =\frac{n!}{(n - x)!} \]

Classical Probability - Permutations and combinations

Combinations

The number of combinations of \(x\) objects chosen from \(n\) is the number of possible selections that can be made

→ order doesn’t matter

\[ C_{x}^n = \frac{P^n_x}{x!} \\ =\frac{n!}{x!(n - x)!} \] and is some application, the following notation is used

\[ \binom{n}{x}= C_{x}^n = \frac{n!}{x!(n - x)!} \]

Example

\[ \binom{5}{2}= C_{2}^5 = \frac{5!}{2!(5 - 1)!}= \frac{5\times4\times3\times2\times1}{2\times1(3\times2\times1)}=10 \]

\[ P(\bar{A}) = 1 - P(A) \]

i.e. \(P(A) + P(\bar{A})=1\)

Complement rule: let A be an event and \(\bar{A}\) its complement, then:

\[ P(\bar{A}) = 1 - P(A) \]

i.e. \(P(A) + P(\bar{A})=1\)

Example

For example, a company is hiring candidates for 4 identical positions. 5 candidates are from India and 3 are from the United States. Assuming that every combination of Indian and American is equally likely to be chosen, what is the probability that at least 1 American will be selected?

Solution

First compute the probability that “no american is selected”, and then using complement rule to compute that “at least 1 American is selected”.

\(P(\bar{A})=\frac{C_{4}^5}{C_{4}^8}=\frac{1}{14}\)

and \(P(A)=1-P(\bar{A})=1-1/14=\frac{13}{14}\)

Addition

Addition rule: the probability of the union of two events is \[ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \]

Example

Consider a standard deck of 52 cards with four suits. Let event A be that the card is an Ace, and event B be that the card is a red suit.

\(P(red \cup ace) = P(red) + P(ace) - P(red \cap ace) = 26/52 + 4/52 - 2/52 = 28/ 52 \ (aprox. 54\%)\)

\(P(red \cap ace)\) → we do not count the two red aces twice.

\[ P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)} \ provided \ that \ P(A)>0 \] i.e. the conditional probability of B given that A has occurred

What is the probability that a car has a CD player, given that it has AC?

\(P(CD \mid AC)= \frac{C(CD \cap AC)}{P(AC)}=\frac{0.2}{0.7}=0.2857\)

Bayes’ Theorem

Bayes’ Theorem is a way of revising probabilities when new information becomes available.

It helps answer:
> “Given that B has occurred, what is the updated probability that A is true?”

\[ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A) P(A)}{P(B)} \]

where:

  • \(P(A)\): prior probability of event A

  • \(P(B \mid A)\): likelihood — probability of B given A

  • \(P(A \mid B)\): posterior — revised probability of A given B

  • \(P(B)\): total probability of B

Bayes’ Theorem

General form

General form of Bayes’ Theorem

The conditional probability of \(E_i\) given \(A\) can be expressed as:

\[ P(E_i \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid E_i) P(E_i)}{P(A) } \] and

\[P(A)=P(A \cap E_1) + P(A \cap E_2) + ... + P(A \cap E_k) \\ = P(A \mid E_1)P(E_1)+P(A \mid E_2)P(E_2)+...+P(A \mid E_k)P(E_k) \]

then

\[ P(E_i \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid E_i) P(E_i)}{P(A \mid E_1) P(E_1)+ P(A \mid E_2) P(E_2)+ ... + P(A \mid E_k) P(E_k)} \\ = \frac{P(A \mid E_i) P(E_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{k} P(A \mid E_j) P(E_j)} \\ \]

where

  • let \(E_1, E_2, ..., E_k\) be \(k\) mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive events

  • \(P(E_i)\) is the prior probability of event \(E_i\)

  • \(A\) is new event whose occurrence leads us to update the probability of \(E_i\) from \(P(E_i)\) to \(P(A \mid E_i)\), assuming \(P(A)>0\)

Bayes’ Theorem

Example

A drilling company estimates a 40% chance of striking oil for their new well. A detailed test is scheduled. Historically:

  • 60% of successful wells had a test: \(P(D \mid S) = 0.6\)

  • 20% of unsuccessful wells had a test: \(P(D \mid U) = 0.2\)

Question: What is the probability the well will be successful given that a detailed test is scheduled?

# Prior probabilities
P_S <- 0.4    # P(Success)
P_U <- 0.6    # P(Unsuccessful)

# Likelihoods
P_D_given_S <- 0.6
P_D_given_U <- 0.2

# Total probability of D
P_D <- P_D_given_S * P_S + P_D_given_U * P_U

# Posterior probability using Bayes' Theorem
P_S_given_D <- (P_D_given_S * P_S) / P_D
P_S_given_D
## [1] 0.6666667

Total probability \[ P(D) = P(D \mid S)P(S) + P(D \mid U)P(U)= (0.6)(0.4) + (0.2)(0.6) = 0.24 + 0.12 = 0.36 \] Bayes’ Theorem

\[ P(S \mid D) = \frac{P(D \mid S)P(S)}{P(D)} = \frac{(0.6)(0.4)}{0.36} = \frac{0.24}{0.36} = 0.667 \]

So the updated probability that the well is successful, given that a test was scheduled, is 0.667, or 66.7%.

This is higher than the prior 40% estimate, because a scheduled test is more common among successful wells.

Probability Distributions (discrete variables)

Discrete vs Continuous

Discrete random variables:

  • Finite or countable values (e.g., number of calls per day, rolling a dice twice)

Continuous random variables:

  • Infinite values in an interval (e.g., temperature).

  • So, possible values are measured in a continuum. (examples: weight, temperature, stock price).

Probability distribution of discrete random variables

Let \(X\) be a discrete random variable and \(x\) be one of its possible values.

The probability that random variable \(X\) takes specific value \(x\) is denoted \(P(X = x)\).

The probability distribution function of a random variable \(x\) is a representation of the probabilities for all the possible outcomes and their corresponding probabilities \(P(x)\)

\[ 0 \leq P(x) \leq 1 \] Individual probabilities should sum to 1. The notation below indicates summation over all possible \(x\) values:

\[ \sum{P(x)} = 1 \]

## Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF)

The **Cumulative Distribution Function** (CDF) denoted as $F(x_0)$ shows the probability that the random variable $X$ is less than or equal to a specific value $x_0$

$$
F(x_0)=P(X \leq x_0)
$$

where the function is evaluated at all values of $x_0$

The derived relationship between between the probability distribution $P(x)$ and the cumulative probability distribution $F(x)$ is 

$$
F(x_0)=\sum_{x \leq x_0} P(x)
$$

# we use x and Px set in the previous example

Expected Value and Variance

Expected value (or mean) of a discrete random variable \(x\) \[ E(X) = \mu = \sum_{j=1}^i x_i p(x_i), \]

Expected variance of a discrete random variable \(x\) \[ \quad Var(X) = \sigma^2= E[(X-\mu]^2)] = \sum_{j=1}^i (x_i - \mu)^2 P(x_i) \\ or \\ \quad Var(X) = \sigma^2= E[(X^2-\mu^2)] = \sum_{j=1}^i x_i^2P(x_i) - \mu^2 \]

Expected standard deviation

Expected standard deviation of a discrete random variable \(x\)

\[ \quad \sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2} = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^i (x_i - \mu)^2 P(x_i)} \]

Binomial Distribution

Among the discreet probability distributions there are the binomial, the Poisson, etc. In this lecture, we will focus on the binomial distribution:

Characteristics

A fixed number of observations, \(n\)

  • e.g., 15 tosses of a coin

Two mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive categories

  • e.g., “success” and “failure”

Constant probability for each observation

  • e.g. Probability of getting a tail is the same each time we toss the coin

Observations are independent

  • i.e. outcome of one observation does not affect the outcome of the other

Binomial Distribution

Probability

Number of sequences with \(x\) successes in \(n\) independent trials with success probability \(p\):

\[ P(X = x) = \frac{n!}{x!(n-x)!}p^x(1-p)^{n - x} \\ = \binom{n}{x} p^x (1 - p)^{n - x} \]

where

  • \(P(x)\) is a the probability of \(x\) ‘successes’ (\(x=0,1,2,3,..,n\))

  • \(n\) trials (our sample here)

  • \(P\) probability of “success” in each independent trial

Binomial Distribution

Mean and Variance

Mean (expected value) \[ \mu = \mathbb{E}[X] = np \qquad \]

Variance and standard deviation \[ \sigma^2 = \text{Var}(X) = np(1 - p) \qquad \\ \sigma = \sqrt{np(1-p)} \\ \] where \(n\) is the sample size, \(P\) is the probability of success, \((1-P)\) is the probability of failure.

Interpretation:

  • Over many repetitions of the experiment, you expect about \(np\) successes per set of \(n\) trials.

  • The variance captures how much the number of successes varies around that average.
