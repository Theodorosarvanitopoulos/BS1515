Estimating an unknown population parameter

A point estimator of a population parameter is a sample statistic (i.e. random variable) used to estimate the corresponding population parameter.

A point estimate is the value of a point estimator used as an estimate of a population parameter.

→ So, we can estimate a population parameter with a sample statistics, i.e, a point estimate.

Example: estimation of the mean weekly wages of graduates in the UK.

Solution: One possible estimator of the population mean is the sample mean.

If the mean of a random sample is £800, then £800 is an estimate of the population weekly wages.

Point Estimates and Population Parameters

We can estimate a population parameter with a sample statistic (point estimate):

Population Parameter Sample Statistic (Point Estimate)
Mean \(\mu\) Sample Mean \(\bar{x}\)
Proportion \(P\) Sample Proportion \(\hat{p}\)

Unbiasedness

A point estimator \(\hat{\theta}\) is said to be an unbiased estimator of the population parameter \(\theta\) if its expected value is equal to that parameter

\[ E(\hat{\theta}) = \theta \]

Examples:

  • Sample mean (\(\bar{x}\)) is an unbiased estimator of population mean (\(\mu\))

  • Sample variance (\(s^2\)) is an unbiased estimator of population variance (\(\sigma^2\))

  • Sample proportion (\(\hat{p}\)) is an unbiased estimator of population proportion (\(P\))

Unbiasedness

A point estimator \(\hat{\theta}\) is said to be an unbiased estimator of the population parameter \(\theta\) if its expected value is equal to that parameter

\[ E(\hat{\theta}) = \theta \]

Examples:

  • Sample mean (\(\bar{x}\)) is an unbiased estimator of population mean (\(\mu\))

  • Sample variance (\(s^2\)) is an unbiased estimator of population variance (\(\sigma^2\))

  • Sample proportion (\(\hat{p}\)) is an unbiased estimator of population proportion (\(P\))

Example where \(\hat{\theta_1}\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\theta\), while \(\hat{\theta_2}\) is biased

Bias

Let \(\hat{\theta}\) be an estimator of \(\theta\)

The bias in \(\hat{\theta}\) is defined as the difference between its mean and \(\theta\)

\[ Bias(\hat{\theta})=E(\hat{\theta})-\theta \] So, the bias of an unbiased estimator is 0.

Efficiency

Suppose there are several unbiased estimators of \(\theta\).

The most efficient estimator or the minimum variance unbiased estimator of \(\theta\) is the unbiased estimator with the smallest variance

Let \(\hat{\theta}_1\) and \(\hat{\theta}_2\) be two unbiased estimators of \(\theta\), based on the same number of observations:

  • \(\hat{\theta}_1\) is more efficient than \(\hat{\theta}_2\) if \(\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}_1)<\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}_2)\)

  • the relative efficiency of \(\hat{\theta}_1\) with respect to \(\hat{\theta}_2\) is the ratio of their variances \(\frac{Var(\hat{\theta}_2)}{Var(\hat{\theta}_1)}\)

Confidence intervals

How much uncertainty is associated with a point estimate of a population parameter?

An interval estimate provides more information about a population characteristic than does a point estimate.

Such interval estimates are called confidence interval (CI) estimates, within which we expect the true population parameter to lie.

Confidence interval estimation

A interval gives a range of values:

  • takes into consideration variation in sample statistics from sample to sample

  • based on observation from 1 sample

  • gives information about closeness to unknown population parameters

  • stated in terms of level of confidence (one can be 100% confident or less)

Confidence interval and confidence level

Let \(\theta\) be an unknown parameter.

If \(P(a<\theta<b)=1-\alpha\), where \(0 \leq \alpha \leq 1\), then the interval from \(a\) to \(b\) is called a \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval of \(\theta\).

The quantity \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) is called the confidence level of the interval.

In repeated samples of the population, the true value of the parameter \(\theta\) would be contained in \(100(1 - \alpha)\%\) of intervals calculated this way.

The confidence interval calculated in this manner is written as \(a < \theta < b\) with \(100(1 - \alpha)\%\) confidence

Confidence level \((1-\alpha)\)

Suppose confidence level = \(95%\), which can also be written as \((1-\alpha)=0.95\).

A relative frequency interpretation:

From repeated samples, \(95\%\) of all the confidence intervals that can be constructed of size \(n\) will contain the unknown true parameter.

A specific interval either will contain or will not contain the true parameter (no probability in specific interval)

General formula of all confidence intervals is:

\[\hat{\theta} \pm ME = \text{Point Estimate} \pm \text{Margin of Error}\]

The value of the margin of error depends on the desired level of confidence

Assumptions:

  • Population variance \(\sigma^2\) is known

  • Population is normally distributed

  • If population is not normal, use large sample (CLT)

Confidence interval estimate for the mean is:

\[ \bar{x} - z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} < \mu < \bar{x} + z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \]

where:

  • \(z_{\alpha/2}\) is the normal distribution value for a probability \(\alpha/2\) in each tail

  • \(\bar{x} - z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\) is the Lower Confidence Limit (LCL)

  • \(\bar{x} + z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\) is the Upper Confidence Limit (LCL)

  • \(z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\) is the Margin of Error or ME

Reducing the margin of error and finding \(z_{\alpha/2}\)

So, the confidence interval can also be written as \(\bar{x} \pm ME\) where \(ME\) is the margin of error

\[ME=z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\]

Reducing the margin of error and finding \(z_{\alpha/2}\)

So, the confidence interval can also be written as \(\bar{x} \pm ME\) where \(ME\) is the margin of error

\[ME=z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\]

The margin of error can be reduced if:

  • the population standard deviation can be reduced (\(\sigma\) ↓) \(\rightarrow\) e.g. reduce variability in measurements

  • the sample size is increased (\(n\) ↑) \(\rightarrow\) more information, more precise inference about the mean

  • the confidence level is decreased \((1-\alpha)\) ↓ \(\rightarrow\) \(95\%\) CI shorter than \(99\%\) CI

Confidence intervals for \(\mu\) (\(\sigma^2\) unknown)

If the population standard deviation \(σ\) is unknown, we can substitute the sample standard deviation, \(s\).

This introduces extra uncertainty, since \(s\) is variable from sample to sample.

So, we use the \(t\) distribution instead of the normal distribution.

William Sealey Gosset developed the Student’s \(t\) distribution in the Guinness Brewery in Dublin.

Student’s \(t\) Distribution

If the sample mean \(\bar{X}\) ~ \(N( \mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n})\) with known population variance \(\sigma^2\), then

\[ Z= \frac{\bar{X}-\mu}{\sigma_{\bar{x}}} = \frac{\bar{X}-\mu}{\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}} \]

Then, we use the Student’s \(t\) Distribution with confidence interval estimates:

\[ \bar{x} - t_{n-1, \ \alpha/2} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} < \mu < \bar{x} + t_{n-1, \ \alpha/2} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \] where:

  • \(t_{n-1, \ \alpha/2}\) is the critical value of the t distribution with \(n-1\) d.f. and an area \(\alpha/2\) in each tail

  • \(\bar{x} - t_{n-1, \ \alpha/2} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\) is the Lower Confidence Limit (LCL)

  • \(\bar{x} + t_{n-1, \ \alpha/2} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\) is the Upper Confidence Limit (LCL)

  • \(t_{n-1, \ \alpha/2} \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\) is the Margin of Error or ME

Student’s \(t\) Distribution

The \(t\) is a family of distributions.

The \(t\) value depends on degrees of freedom (d.f.), which is the number of observations that are free to vary after the sample mean has been calculated:

\[ d.f.=n-1 \]

\(t\) → \(Z\) as \(n\) increases

\(t\) distributions are bell shaped and symmetric, but have ‘fatter’ tails than the normal distribution (with infinite d.f.)

Confidence intervals for population proportion

An interval estimate for the population proportion \(P\) can be calculated by adding an allowance for uncertainty to the sample proportion \(\hat{p}\)

The distribution of the sample proportion is approximately normal if the sample size is large, with standard deviation

\[ \sigma_P=\sqrt{\frac{P(1-P)}{n}} \]

and we estimate this with sample data \(\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}}\)

Confidence intervals for population proportion

An interval estimate for the population proportion \(P\) can be calculated by adding an allowance for uncertainty to the sample proportion \(\hat{p}\)

The distribution of the sample proportion is approximately normal if the sample size is large, with standard deviation

\[ \sigma_P=\sqrt{\frac{P(1-P)}{n}} \]

and we estimate this with sample data \(\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}}\)

The confidence interval for population proportion is given by

\[ \hat{p} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}} \]

where:

  • \(z_{\alpha/2}\) is the standard normal value for the level of confidence desired

  • \(\hat{p}\) is the sample proportion and \(n\) is the sample size

  • \(z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}}\) is the Margin of Error

  • \(nP(1-P) > 5\)
